Decoding Cat Meows: What Different Sounds Mean

Cat's Meow Cat Rescue · May 10, 2025

As our rescue's name suggests, we at Cat's Meow Cat Rescue have a special appreciation for feline vocalizations. Cats have an extraordinarily diverse vocal repertoire — researchers have identified over a dozen distinct sounds — and each one communicates something specific. Learning to interpret these sounds deepens your relationship with your cat and helps you respond to their needs more effectively.

The Meow: A Language Made for Humans

Here is a surprising fact: adult cats rarely meow at each other. Meowing is a behavior that domestic cats developed specifically to communicate with humans. Kittens meow to their mothers, but wild adult cats are mostly silent with one another. Your cat's meows are quite literally a language they created for you.

Common meow variations and their typical meanings:

  • Short, high-pitched meow: A standard greeting. "Hello, you are home."
  • Multiple short meows in a row: Excitement. "I am really happy to see you."
  • Mid-pitch meow: A request. Usually for food, attention, or an open door.
  • Long, drawn-out meow: A demand. Your cat wants something and is becoming insistent.
  • Low-pitched meow or growl-meow: A complaint. Something is wrong or displeasing.
  • High-pitched, loud meow: Pain, distress, or alarm. Investigate immediately.

Purring: More Complex Than You Think

Most people associate purring with contentment, and that is usually accurate. A cat curled in your lap with a soft, rhythmic purr is expressing deep satisfaction. However, cats also purr when they are in pain, anxious, or seriously ill. This self-soothing mechanism may promote healing — the vibration frequency of purring (25-150 Hz) has been shown to promote bone density and tissue repair.

Context tells you which kind of purr you are hearing. A purring cat who is eating normally, relaxed, and seeking affection is content. A purring cat who is withdrawn, not eating, or showing other signs of distress needs veterinary attention.

Chirps, Trills, and Chattering

These delightful sounds reveal different aspects of your cat's inner world:

  • Chirps and trills: Short, rising sounds that mother cats use to communicate with kittens. When your cat chirps at you, they are saying "come here" or "follow me." Many cats chirp when greeting their favorite person.
  • Chattering or chittering: That rapid teeth-clicking sound cats make while watching birds through the window. Behaviorists debate whether this is frustration at unreachable prey, excitement, or a predatory jaw-movement rehearsal. Whatever the cause, it is harmless and entertaining to observe.
  • Trilling: A rolling, musical sound that combines a meow and a purr. This is a purely positive vocalization, expressing happiness and affection.

Warning Sounds

Some vocalizations are clear signals to keep your distance:

  • Hissing: A defensive sound that means "back off." A hissing cat is frightened, not aggressive. Give them space immediately.
  • Growling: A low, sustained rumble warning of potential aggression. Do not approach or attempt to pick up a growling cat.
  • Yowling: A long, loud, warbling cry. In unspayed or unneutered cats, yowling is related to mating. In spayed or neutered cats, it can indicate disorientation, pain, or cognitive decline in older cats. Persistent yowling in a senior cat warrants a veterinary visit.

When Vocalization Changes

Pay attention to changes in your cat's vocal patterns. A quiet cat who suddenly becomes vocal, or a chatty cat who goes silent, may be communicating a health issue. Increased vocalization is common in cats with hyperthyroidism, high blood pressure, or cognitive dysfunction — all treatable conditions that your veterinarian can diagnose.
