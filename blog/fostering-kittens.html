Fostering Neonatal Kittens: A Beginner's Guide

Cat's Meow Cat Rescue · February 8, 2025

Neonatal kittens — those under four weeks old — are among the most vulnerable animals in any rescue system. Without their mother, they cannot regulate their own body temperature, eat solid food, or even go to the bathroom without help. At Cat's Meow Cat Rescue, bottle baby fosters are true lifesavers, and while the work is demanding, it is also one of the most rewarding experiences a cat lover can have.

Understanding Neonatal Kitten Needs

Kittens are born with their eyes closed, their ears folded, and almost no ability to stay warm on their own. During the first two weeks of life, maintaining warmth is the single most critical factor in their survival. A heating pad set on low beneath half their enclosure (so they can move away if too warm), supplemented with soft blankets, will mimic the warmth their mother would provide.

By two weeks, their eyes begin to open. By three weeks, they start to wobble around on unsteady legs. Each day brings rapid changes, and part of the joy of fostering neonates is watching these milestones unfold in real time.

Bottle Feeding Basics

Neonatal kittens need to be bottle-fed a kitten milk replacer (KMR) formula every two to three hours around the clock for the first two weeks. Here is a general feeding schedule:

  • Birth to 1 week: Feed every 2 hours, including overnight, approximately 2-6 ml per feeding
  • 1 to 2 weeks: Feed every 2-3 hours, approximately 6-10 ml per feeding
  • 2 to 3 weeks: Feed every 3-4 hours, approximately 10-14 ml per feeding
  • 3 to 4 weeks: Feed every 4-5 hours and begin introducing gruel (formula mixed with wet kitten food)

Always feed kittens on their bellies, never on their backs like a human baby. Aspiration pneumonia from formula entering the lungs is a serious and potentially fatal risk. Cat's Meow provides formula, bottles, and nipples to all bottle baby fosters, along with detailed feeding instructions.

Stimulation and Hygiene

Kittens under three weeks old cannot urinate or defecate on their own. After every feeding, you will need to gently stimulate their genital area with a warm, damp cotton ball or soft cloth to mimic the mother cat's tongue. This process takes about 30 seconds and should produce results quickly. Once kittens are around three to four weeks old, they will begin using a shallow litter box on their own.

Keep your neonatal kittens' bedding clean and dry. Soiled bedding can lead to skin infections and chilling. Weigh your kittens daily on a kitchen scale — consistent weight gain is the best indicator that they are thriving. Any weight loss or failure to gain should be reported to your Cat's Meow foster coordinator immediately.

Common Health Concerns

Neonatal kittens can decline quickly, so vigilance is key. Watch for these warning signs:

  • Refusal to eat or weak sucking reflex
  • Crying constantly or appearing lethargic
  • Diarrhea or bloated belly
  • Discharge from eyes or nose
  • Feeling cold to the touch despite heat support

If you notice any of these symptoms, contact Cat's Meow right away. We have relationships with veterinary clinics in the Milwaukie and Gladstone area that can see urgent foster cases quickly.

The Weaning Transition

Around four weeks of age, kittens begin the transition from formula to solid food. Start by offering a slurry of wet kitten food mixed with formula in a shallow dish. Expect a mess — kittens will walk through it, sit in it, and wear more than they eat at first. Gradually reduce the formula ratio over the next two to three weeks until they are eating wet food independently.

By six to eight weeks, your foster kittens will be eating on their own, using the litter box reliably, and playing with boundless energy. Once they reach two pounds, they can be spayed or neutered and made available for adoption. Those sleepless nights of two-hour feedings will feel entirely worth it when you see your bottle babies head home with loving families.
