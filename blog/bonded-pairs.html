Adopting Bonded Pairs: Why Two Cats Are Better Than One

Cat's Meow Cat Rescue · May 14, 2025

At Cat's Meow Cat Rescue, some of our cats come to us as inseparable pairs: siblings who grew up together, friends who bonded in foster care, or adult cats who simply cannot imagine life without each other. When we list a bonded pair, we mean that separating them would cause real emotional distress. Adopting both is not just a nice thing to do. It is the right thing to do. And it comes with more benefits than you might expect.

What Makes a Bonded Pair

Not every two cats who share a space are bonded. A true bonded pair shows specific behaviors that indicate deep attachment:

  • They sleep curled up together regularly, not just occasionally.
  • They groom each other, especially around the head and ears.
  • They show signs of anxiety or distress when separated, such as excessive vocalization, pacing, or refusing to eat.
  • They play together, eat together, and seek each other out throughout the day.

Our foster families observe these dynamics carefully before we designate any cats as a bonded pair. When we say two cats need to go home together, it is based on weeks of behavioral observation.

The Benefits of Adopting Two

While doubling the cats might seem like doubling the work, the reality is often the opposite:

  • Built-in companionship: Bonded pairs entertain each other while you are at work, reducing boredom and the destructive behaviors that can come with it.
  • Easier adjustment: A cat entering a new home with their bonded partner experiences significantly less stress. They have a familiar face, a familiar scent, and a source of comfort during the transition.
  • More exercise: Two cats chase, wrestle, and play together, keeping each other physically active without requiring as much hands-on play from you.
  • Emotional stability: Cats in bonded pairs tend to display fewer anxiety-related behaviors like over-grooming, excessive meowing, or litter box issues.

Common Concerns About Adopting a Pair

"Is it really that much more expensive?" The incremental cost of a second cat is modest. You will need an additional litter box and slightly more food, but most other costs remain the same. Many rescues, including Cat's Meow, offer reduced adoption fees for bonded pairs to make it easier for families to keep them together.

"I do not have enough space." Two bonded cats actually need less space than two unrelated cats because they share territory willingly. A one-bedroom apartment is perfectly adequate for a bonded pair, especially with vertical space like a cat tree or wall-mounted shelves.

"What if they stop getting along?" Genuinely bonded cats maintain their bond throughout their lives. It is different from two cats who are simply tolerating each other. When the bond is real, it is permanent.

What Happens When Bonded Pairs Are Separated

Separating a bonded pair can have serious consequences. Cats may stop eating, become withdrawn or lethargic, develop stress-related health issues like urinary problems, or become aggressive. Some separated cats vocalize constantly, calling for their missing companion. This is not anthropomorphizing. These are documented behavioral responses to the loss of a social bond.

At Cat's Meow, we have seen the heartbreak that separation causes, which is why we are committed to placing bonded pairs together, even if it means they wait longer for the right home.

Ready to Double the Love?

If you have room in your heart and home for two cats, adopting a bonded pair is one of the most meaningful things you can do. You are giving two animals the chance to stay together and thrive.
