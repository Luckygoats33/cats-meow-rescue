Caring for Senior Cats: A Guide for Aging Felines

Cat's Meow Cat Rescue · June 14, 2025

Cats are living longer than ever, thanks to advances in veterinary medicine and a better understanding of feline nutrition. A cat is generally considered senior at age 11 and geriatric at age 15, though many cats remain active and healthy well into their late teens and even early twenties. At Cat's Meow Cat Rescue, we have a special place in our hearts for senior cats. They are often the hardest to adopt out, yet they make some of the most rewarding companions. Here is how to give your aging cat the best possible quality of life.

Health Screening and Veterinary Care

Senior cats should visit the veterinarian every six months rather than annually. Cats age faster than humans, and six months for a senior cat is roughly equivalent to two human years. Semi-annual visits allow your vet to catch developing problems early. A typical senior wellness visit includes:

  • Complete blood work to check kidney function, liver enzymes, blood sugar, thyroid levels, and red and white blood cell counts
  • Urinalysis to detect early kidney disease, diabetes, or urinary tract infections
  • Blood pressure measurement, as hypertension is common in older cats and can damage the eyes, kidneys, heart, and brain
  • Weight tracking to identify gradual loss or gain that could signal underlying disease
  • Dental evaluation, since dental disease is pervasive in senior cats and causes significant pain

Nutrition for Aging Cats

Older cats have different nutritional needs than their younger counterparts. Senior-specific diets are formulated to be easier to digest, lower in phosphorus to support kidney health, and enriched with joint-supporting nutrients like glucosamine and omega-3 fatty acids. Key dietary considerations include:

  • Maintaining adequate protein intake to prevent muscle wasting, which is common in aging cats
  • Ensuring sufficient moisture through wet food, as older cats are more prone to dehydration
  • Monitoring calorie intake, since senior cats tend to be less active but still need nutrient-dense meals
  • Adding water fountains to encourage drinking, as many cats prefer moving water

Comfort and Mobility

Arthritis affects a surprisingly large number of senior cats — some studies suggest up to 90 percent of cats over age 12 show radiographic evidence of degenerative joint disease. Signs include reluctance to jump, stiffness after resting, difficulty using the litter box, and reduced grooming. You can improve your senior cat's comfort with:

  • Low-entry litter boxes that do not require climbing over a high wall
  • Ramps or pet stairs to favorite perches and furniture
  • Heated beds or pads that soothe aching joints, especially during Oregon's cool, damp winters
  • Soft bedding in multiple locations throughout the home
  • Non-slip surfaces on slippery floors to help with traction

Talk to your veterinarian about pain management options. Joint supplements, anti-inflammatory medications, and even acupuncture can make a meaningful difference in your senior cat's mobility and comfort.

Cognitive Changes and Behavior

Just like humans, some cats experience cognitive decline as they age. Signs of feline cognitive dysfunction syndrome include disorientation, changes in sleep patterns, increased vocalization (especially at night), staring blankly, forgetting learned behaviors, and changes in interactions with family members. While there is no cure, you can support a cat with cognitive changes by maintaining a consistent routine, keeping the environment predictable, providing nightlights in dark areas, and discussing supplements or medications with your vet.

Why Senior Cats Deserve Love

Senior cats at Cat's Meow Cat Rescue are already spayed or neutered, fully vetted, and past the rambunctious kitten stage. They tend to be calmer, more affectionate, and deeply grateful for a comfortable home. Many of our senior cats are surrendered due to their owner's life changes, not because of any fault of the cat. If you have room in your heart and home, adopting a senior cat is one of the most compassionate things you can do. These golden-year companions have so much love left to give.
