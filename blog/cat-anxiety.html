Signs of Anxiety in Cats and How to Help

Cat's Meow Cat Rescue · June 21, 2025

Cats are masters at hiding discomfort, which makes anxiety one of the most underdiagnosed conditions in feline companions. Many behaviors that owners attribute to stubbornness, spite, or personality quirks are actually symptoms of an anxious cat. Recognizing the signs of anxiety early allows you to intervene before the stress compounds into more serious behavioral or medical problems.

Recognizing the Signs

Cat anxiety rarely looks like human anxiety. Cats express stress through behavior changes that can be subtle and easy to overlook. Watch for these indicators:

  • Excessive grooming: Overgrooming to the point of creating bald patches or skin sores, often on the belly, inner legs, or flanks. This is a self-soothing behavior that can become compulsive.
  • Litter box avoidance: Urinating or defecating outside the box, especially in places associated with the owner (beds, laundry, shoes). This is often misread as revenge but is typically a stress response.
  • Hiding more than usual: Spending increasing amounts of time under furniture or in closets, especially if the cat was previously social.
  • Appetite changes: Either eating significantly less or stress-eating more than usual.
  • Excessive vocalization: Increased meowing, yowling, or crying, particularly at night or when left alone.
  • Aggression: Swatting, biting, or hissing when approached, especially if this is new behavior.
  • Displacement behaviors: Sudden intense grooming during a stressful moment, rapid tail flicking, or dilated pupils in a calm environment.

Common Causes of Feline Anxiety

Understanding what triggers your cat's stress helps you address the root cause:

  • Environmental changes: Moving, remodeling, new furniture, or rearranging rooms. Cats build mental maps of their territory, and changes disrupt their sense of security.
  • Schedule changes: A new job, shift change, or travel schedule can unsettle cats who rely on routine.
  • Separation anxiety: Despite their independent reputation, many cats become anxious when their primary person leaves. This is especially common in cats who were orphaned young or have been rehomed multiple times.
  • Inter-cat conflict: Subtle tension between household cats — silent staring, blocking access to resources, or one cat always yielding space — creates chronic stress.
  • Outside cats: Stray or feral cats visible through windows can cause intense territorial anxiety. This is common in Oregon neighborhoods, especially in areas near natural spaces around Milwaukie and Gladstone.

Creating a Low-Stress Environment

The foundation of treating feline anxiety is environmental enrichment and resource security. Practical steps include:

  1. Vertical space: Cat trees, wall shelves, and high perches give cats a sense of control over their environment. Anxious cats feel safer when they can observe from above.
  2. Resource distribution: In multi-cat homes, spread food bowls, water stations, litter boxes, and resting spots throughout the house so no cat has to compete or pass through another cat's territory.
  3. Routine: Feed, play, and interact at the same times each day. Predictability reduces anxiety significantly.
  4. Safe spaces: Every cat needs at least one retreat — a covered bed, a high shelf, a room with a cat door — where they will not be disturbed by other pets, children, or visitors.
  5. Pheromone diffusers: Synthetic feline facial pheromone products (such as Feliway) can reduce anxiety in some cats. Plug them in near your cat's favorite resting spots.

When to Talk to Your Veterinarian

If environmental changes do not improve your cat's anxiety within a few weeks, or if the anxiety is severe, consult your veterinarian. Medical causes must be ruled out first — hyperthyroidism, pain, urinary issues, and gastrointestinal problems can all produce anxiety-like symptoms.

For cats with diagnosed anxiety disorders, your veterinarian may recommend anti-anxiety medication combined with behavioral modification. Medication is not a failure — it is a tool that helps your cat feel safe enough to learn new coping patterns.
